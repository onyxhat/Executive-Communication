# Revision Patterns: What Executives Cut and Add

Read this when revising a technically-authored draft for a senior audience, or when a draft has come back marked up and you are trying to generalize the feedback rather than apply it once.

These patterns are drawn from observed edits made by senior leaders to drafts written by technical authors. They are consistent enough to apply predictively: you can make most of these edits before the draft goes out.

## 1. The defensive apparatus

**Pattern: sections that pre-empt objections get deleted.**

Technical authors anticipate pushback and answer it in the document. A section correcting a wrong figure in circulation, a paragraph explaining why an apparent increase is not really an increase, a counterfactual showing what the alternative would cost. These are the first things a senior editor removes.

Three reasons, and they compound:

- Repeating a wrong number in writing gives it a citable home. The next person to quote the document may quote the number you were refuting.
- Written rebuttal reads as defensive in a way that the same point made verbally does not.
- The document usually circulates further than the misunderstanding did. You are explaining an internal confusion to people who never had it.

**What to do instead:** state the correct figure as fact, in the position where a figure belongs, and let it stand unargued. Flag the misperception to the sponsor separately so they can handle it in the room. Objections are handled by people, not by paragraphs.

## 2. Emphasis and instruction

**Pattern: inline bold disappears, and so do sentences that tell the reader how to read.**

Two related cuts:

- **Bold inside body sentences.** Emphasis migrates to structure: headings, bullets, a terms block. Bolding a phrase mid-sentence tells the reader what to conclude before they have weighed the evidence, which in a document seeking approval reads as selling.
- **Reader instructions.** "Read the bottom row across." "Note that this is the key point." "The scope comparison matters just as much." If the structure is right, the reader does not need directing. If it is wrong, directing them does not fix it.

Also cut: closing lines that reach for resonance. "This renewal covers the environment we actually operate, before the busiest season we have to protect" is good writing and it still comes out. Persuasive cadence is a signal of advocacy, and advocacy is what the reader is evaluating, not what they want performed at them.

## 3. Precision calibration

**Pattern: some numbers get more precise, others get deliberately vaguer.**

This is the pattern most often misread as inconsistency. It is not. Precision is calibrated to how settled the number is and to who else has a claim on it.

| Kind of number | Direction of edit |
|---|---|
| Settled commercial terms | Kept exact, often promoted to a terms block |
| Quantified capability claims | Kept exact, almost never cut |
| Estimates you own | Kept, with the basis or source attached |
| Items in live negotiation with a counterparty | Figure removed, item and direction retained |
| Net positions that depend on several things landing | Softened to a defensible claim |
| Derived ratios built on other numbers | Cut |

Two specific moves worth naming:

**Open negotiations lose their figure.** A dollar amount still being negotiated becomes an anchor once written down. "A $162K exposure" becomes "potential exposure across the term." You lose precision and gain room.

**Net claims get hedged.** "Close to cost neutral" becomes "offsets much of the increase." The first is a promise that fails if one component slips; the second is true under every outcome. The persuasive cost is near zero and the downside protection is real.

**Source qualifiers get added.** "99.7% of guest session volume" becomes "99.7% of guest session volume in the source estimate." Senior readers add attribution the author left off, because they are the one who will be asked where it came from.

## 4. Scope of the number

**Pattern: the headline number gets bigger, and the time horizon gets longer.**

Technical drafts lead with the smallest defensible framing of the cost. An incremental increase rather than a total. A percentage rather than a dollar figure. Year one rather than the full term. This is a reasonable instinct about sticker shock and it is consistently reversed.

Senior editors lead with the total obligation and extend tables across the full term. The reasoning: the approval body is approving the total, so the smaller number is not the decision. And a reader who finds the total late goes back through the rest of the document looking for what else was framed favorably. That suspicion costs more than the large number ever did.

**Apply it forward:** find the largest true number in your draft. If it is not in the first three sentences, move it there.

## 5. Fact and benefit separation

**Pattern: justification clauses get trimmed off individual facts and consolidated elsewhere.**

Technical drafts interleave: each capability bullet carries its own "which supports X" tail. Senior edits split these apart, leaving bare quantified facts in one list and collecting the value framing into separate outcome lists, often segmented by audience.

- *Before:* "2x expansion of application deployment coverage, supporting more scalable applications and digital services as guest demand and delivery velocity increase"
- *After:* "2x expansion of application deployment coverage to support more scalable applications and digital services." Plus, separately, a short list including "Protect digital revenue" and "Improve guest experience."

Why this works: one bullet does one job, so each list scans fast. And the outcome list functions as portfolio tagging, letting a reader connect the request to strategic priorities without reading the detail.

Note what does **not** get cut: the quantified capability claims themselves. 2x, 4x, 100% from 20%, 42%. These survive nearly verbatim and are the most durable contribution a technical author makes to the document. The numbers are what the author uniquely knows.

## 6. Audience width

**Pattern: content gets added for readers the author did not picture.**

Senior editors consistently add:

- **A one-sentence definition of the subject.** The author wrote for the working group. The document routes to Legal, Procurement, and Finance, where somebody is reading it cold.
- **Answers to standing functional questions.** Whether it was competitively bid. How it is accounted for. What caps future increases. These come from reviewers who were never in the technical conversation and who each hold a veto.
- **The organization's name in place of "we."** In a document that may reach outside counsel or an external party, the entity gets named.

Meanwhile, internal shorthand comes out: project codenames, team-local abbreviations, tool names that mean nothing outside the group.

**The generalization:** list every function that will read the document before drafting, not just the decision-maker. Each one brings a standing question, and an unanswered standing question is a delay.

## 7. The ask

**Pattern: work assigned to the approver gets moved back to the team.**

- *Before:* "endorsement to proceed, and confirmation of the retirement target so the offset can be booked"
- *After:* "approve proceeding... and allows the team to validate the retirement targets before booking the offsets"

The draft asked the executive to go find something out. The revision left them one binary decision and moved the verification back where it belongs. An ask with two parts, one of which is homework, converts a fast yes into a scheduling problem.

Related and equally consistent: **"we have already declined X" gets cut.** Telling an approver that a decision was made without them, inside a document asking them to approve, steps on their authority. Present alternatives as considered and not recommended, or leave them out.

## 8. Applying these to your own draft

Run this pass before sending, in this order. It is roughly the order a senior editor works in.

1. **Find the largest true number.** Is it in the first three sentences? If not, move it.
2. **Delete every section that argues with something.** Corrections, rebuttals, counterfactuals, "on the perception of." State the right facts and let them stand.
3. **Strip inline bold and reader instructions.** Move emphasis into structure.
4. **Sort every number** into settled, estimated, or under negotiation. Make each one as precise as its category allows and no more.
5. **Split facts from benefits.** Bare quantified claims in one list, value framing in another.
6. **List the functions that will read this.** Add the one sentence each of them needs.
7. **Check the ask.** Is it one decision, with no task attached?

Most of this is subtractive, which is why it is fast. The additions are narrow and predictable: a definition, the governance answers, and the total.
