# Approval and Governance Documents

Read this when the deliverable asks a body to approve something: a contract, renewal, purchase, capital request, exception, or budget line. These documents follow different rules from status updates and recommendations, and the difference is not stylistic. It is structural.

## 1. Why these are different

A status update has one audience and one purpose. An approval document is read by several bodies at once, each of which arrives with its own standing questions and its own veto. Finance asks how it is accounted for and what the total commitment is. Procurement asks whether it was competitively bid. Legal asks about term, liability, and renewal mechanics. The business sponsor asks what it buys.

Any one of them can stall the approval by asking a question the document does not answer. So the completeness bar is higher than for any other executive format, and the usual instinct to compress works against you.

**Approval documents are often longer than the draft they replace.** Executive editing here is not compression. It is reallocation: argument comes out, governance completeness goes in.

## 2. Find the template first

Most organizations already have a required form for this: a contract Executive Summary, a capital request, a purchase justification. Ask whether one exists before drafting a single sentence.

This matters more than it sounds. A well-argued document in the wrong container does not get accepted on its merits. It gets poured into the correct container by whoever owns that container, and reasoning that has no slot in the form is lost in the transfer. Writing into the right template from the start means the author decides what survives instead of the reviewer.

If a template exists, treat its section headings as fixed and its blank fields as questions that must be visibly answered or visibly routed. If none exists, use the structure in section 7.

## 3. The governance questions

These are the questions that stall approvals. They rarely come from the business, which is why technically-authored drafts routinely omit them.

| Question | Who asks | What the document must say |
|---|---|---|
| Was this competitively bid? | Procurement | Yes with results, or no with a defensible reason |
| How is it accounted for? | Finance | OpEx or CapEx, and whether accounting has validated it |
| What is the total obligation? | Finance | Full contract value across the full term |
| What is the term and what happens at renewal? | Legal, Finance | Length, auto-renewal, and any cap on increases |
| What are we exposed to if this goes wrong? | Legal | Commercial and legal risks, named plainly |
| What happens if we do not approve? | Sponsor | The consequence, stated without melodrama |
| Who owns delivery? | All | A named team or role |

Answer them even when the answer is unflattering. "An RFP was not conducted because IT has limited capacity to evaluate and transition to an alternative platform" is a complete answer. Silence on the same point is an open question that comes back as a delay.

## 4. State the total obligation

Lead with the full commitment, not the incremental one. If the request is three years at $850K, $1.0M, and $1.15M, the headline number is $3.0M over 36 months.

The temptation runs the other way. A year-one delta of $190K is easier to defend than $3.0M, so drafts tend to lead with the delta and let the total emerge later, or not at all. Resist this for two reasons. Approval bodies approve total obligation, so the smaller number is not the thing being decided. And a reader who discovers late that the total was never on the page will re-read everything else looking for what else was managed. That credibility loss costs more than the sticker shock it avoided.

The same logic applies to tables. Show every year of the term, not just year one.

## 5. Handling open items and estimates

Approval documents get quoted back to you. Calibrate precision to how settled each number actually is.

- **Settled commercial terms:** state exactly. Contract value, discount, term length, caps.
- **Estimates you own:** state with the basis attached. "Expected reduction in Splunk Cloud spend, scoped in the migration project."
- **Items still being negotiated with a counterparty:** name the item and its direction, but do not publish a figure. A number written down before it is settled becomes an anchor, and people will hold you to it while you are still negotiating.
- **Items requiring another team's sign-off:** leave the question visibly open and routed. An unanswered checkbox with a named owner is an action item. A guessed answer is a liability.

Avoid claiming a net position you cannot guarantee. "This renewal is close to cost neutral" is a promise that fails if one offset slips. "Expected reductions that will offset much of the increase" is defensible under every outcome and costs almost nothing in persuasive force.

Attribute estimates that came from someone else. "99.7% of guest session volume in the source estimate" is honest in a way that the bare figure is not.

## 6. What to leave out

- **Rebuttals of internal misinformation.** If a wrong number is circulating, do not correct it in the document. State the right number as fact and let the sponsor handle the misperception verbally. Repeating a wrong figure in writing gives it a citable home and makes the document read as defensive.
- **Counterfactual forecasts.** "It would cost $1.43M if we did nothing" is rhetoric. It is unverifiable and invites a challenge you cannot settle in the meeting.
- **Non-recurring items inside run-rate tables.** A one-time credit sitting in a table of annual spend distorts the year-over-year comparison even when every individual figure is accurate. Report it separately.
- **Derived ratios.** Keep the headline commercial term. Cut the arithmetic you did on top of it. "42% discount" stays; "each dollar buys 72% more volume" goes.
- **Decisions already taken.** "We have declined the alternative structure" tells the approver a choice was made without them. Present alternatives as considered and not recommended, or omit them.
- **Tasks for the approver.** The ask is a decision. If someone needs to go verify a number, that is your team's work, not theirs.

## 7. Section-by-section structure

Use this when no organizational template exists. Where one does exist, use its headings instead and map this content into them.

```
[Vendor / subject and contract type]

Summary and business rationale
  [One sentence defining what this is, for readers outside the working group.]
  [What is being requested: structure, total value, term.]
  [What the change buys, in two or three sentences.]
  [Why this path and not an alternative, including competitive bidding.]

Key terms
  Term length, structure, total contract value, commercial position,
  cost containment, accounting treatment, competitive bid status.

Operational or financial benefits
  [Short prose framing, then the multi-year spend table.]

Key benefits
  [Quantified capability bullets. Fact only, no justification tails.]

Business outcomes / IT outcomes
  [Two short verb-first lists, unquantified, mapped to strategic priorities.]

Legal and commercial risks
  [Named plainly. Direction stated, open negotiations not quantified.]

Accounting considerations
  [Treatment, and whether it has been validated, with the question left
   visibly open if it has not.]

Recommendation and ask
  [One decision, stated as a decision.]
```

**On the two outcome lists.** Splitting business outcomes from IT outcomes is worth the duplication. Two audiences scan for different things, and the lists work as portfolio tags: they let a reader place the request against a strategic roadmap without reading the detail.

**On the capability bullets.** Keep the quantified claim and cut the trailing "which supports X" clause. The value framing belongs in the outcomes lists, not attached to each fact. One bullet, one job.
